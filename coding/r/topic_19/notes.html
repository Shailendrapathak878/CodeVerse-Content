1. Introduction to Statistics, Population & Sampling

Statistics is the mathematical science of collecting, organizing, exploring, analyzing, interpreting, and presenting empirical data. In AI and Data Science, statistics provides the rigorous foundation for model validation, feature selection, anomaly detection, and decision-making under uncertainty.

The Two Major Branches:
  • Descriptive Statistics: Summarizes and describes properties of observed datasets (mean, median, standard deviation, quartiles, tables). No generalization beyond the data at hand.
  • Inferential Statistics: Uses sample data to draw conclusions, test scientific hypotheses, and make probability estimates about an entire unobserved population.
2. Measures of Central Tendency (Mean, Median, Mode, Weighted Mean)

Central Tendency identifies the center or single typical value representing an entire distribution of numbers. Consider our standardized benchmark dataset of student marks:

R Script

1. Mean (Arithmetic Average):
mean(marks) = 67.78
Sum of all observations divided by count. Highly sensitive to extreme outliers.
2. Median (50th Percentile):
median(marks) = 65.00
Middle value of ordered data. Robust and resistant against severe skewness and outliers.
3. Statistical Mode:
Mode = 65 (freq = 2)
Most frequent value. Base R's mode() returns storage type; use custom table() helper.
3. Measures of Dispersion & Variability

Two datasets can share identical means while exhibiting vastly different spread (e.g. [68, 70, 72] vs [40, 70, 100] both have mean = 70). Dispersion quantifies variability, consistency, and risk.

Metric R Function Mathematical Formula Key Characteristic
Range diff(range(x)) Max - Min Fastest calculation; extremely sensitive to single outlier
Variance var(x) s² = Σ(x - x̄)² / (n - 1) Unbiased sample variance using Bessel's correction; squared units
Standard Deviation sd(x) s = √s² Typical spread in original measurement units; widely used in ML
Interquartile Range IQR(x) Q3 - Q1 Spread of central 50%; robust against outliers and non-normal tails
Coefficient of Variation sd(x) / mean(x) * 100 (s / x̄) * 100% Unitless percentage; compares volatility across different scales
4. Quantiles, Percentiles & Distribution Shapes

Quantiles divide sorted observations into equal-sized subgroups. Quartiles divide data into 4 parts (Q1 25%, Q2 50%, Q3 75%), deciles into 10 parts, and percentiles into 100 parts.

Distribution Shapes: Real-world data curves vary significantly in symmetry and tail weight. Knowing the shape informs whether parametric or non-parametric tests should be applied.

5. Frequency Distributions, Missing Data & Grouped Summaries

Categorical features and discrete ratings are summarized through frequency counts, proportions, and cumulative totals.

R Script
R 
# Grouped Descriptive Statistics via dplyr
library(dplyr)
student_summary %
  group_by(department) %>%
  summarise(
    mean_score = mean(marks, na.rm = TRUE),
    median_score = median(marks, na.rm = TRUE),
    std_dev = sd(marks, na.rm = TRUE),
    count = n()
  )
6. Relationships Between Variables (Covariance, Correlation, Heatmaps)

Covariance determines whether two numerical variables rise or fall together, but depends on units. Correlation scales covariance to [-1.0, +1.0], measuring the strength and direction of the linear or monotonic relationship.

7. Inferential Statistics, Hypothesis Testing & P-Values

Hypothesis Testing provides an objective mathematical framework to decide whether an observed experimental effect or difference in sample data is statistically significant, or whether it could easily be explained by random sampling chance.

Crucial Scientific Rule: A p-value is NOT the probability that H0 is true! It is the probability of observing data at least as extreme as your sample assuming H0 is true. Never claim that failing to reject H0 "proves" there is zero effect.

11. 30 Common Beginner Mistakes & Statistical Pitfalls

1. Using mode() for statistical mode: mode(x) returns object storage type ("numeric"). names(which.max(table(x))).

2. Forgetting na.rm = TRUE: mean(c(1, NA, 3)) returns NA. mean(c(1, NA, 3), na.rm = TRUE) returns 2.

3. Using mean on heavily skewed data: Reporting average salary when executives distort the mean. Use the median.

4. Forgetting to sort before finding median manually: Median is the middle of ordered data, not unsorted data.

5. Confusing variance and standard deviation: Reporting variance in squared units without taking the square root.

6. Assuming R var() is population variance: R divides by (n - 1) for sample variance, not N.

7. Confusing percentiles with percentages: Scoring at the 90th percentile means outperforming 90% of students, not scoring 90%.

8. Confusing covariance with correlation: Covariance magnitude is scale-dependent; use correlation for standardized [-1, 1].

9. Believing correlation proves causation: Lurking confounders can cause high correlation without causal link.

10. Applying Pearson to nonlinear monotonic data: Pearson assumes linearity; use Spearman rank correlation instead.

11. Misinterpreting p-value: Claiming p-value is the probability that H0 is true. It is P(Data | H0).

12. "p > 0.05 proves no effect": Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. We fail to reject H0.

13. Confusing statistical significance with practical impact: A tiny difference in 1,000,000 rows can be p < 0.001 but clinically trivial.

14. Using paired t-test on independent groups: Paired tests require repeated measures on the exact same subjects.

15. Using independent t-test on before-after data: Discarding subject matching reduces test power.

16. Ignoring small cell counts in Chi-Square: When expected counts are < 5, use fisher.test().

17. Thinking significant ANOVA means all groups differ: It only proves at least one differs. Run TukeyHSD().

18. Thinking non-significant Shapiro test proves normality: Small samples lack power to detect mild non-normality.

19. Calculating CV when mean is near zero: Dividing by near-zero inflates CV toward infinity.

20. Blindly deleting missing rows: Deleting incomplete cases can introduce massive sampling bias.

21. Confusing population parameter with sample statistic: μ vs x̄ and σ vs s.

22. Using accuracy on imbalanced datasets: 99% accuracy by predicting only the majority class is useless. Use F1-score.

23. Evaluating models on training data: Causes massive overfitting optimism; always test on holdout data.

24. Forgetting cor() needs use = 'complete.obs': cor(df) returns NAs if any row has missing values.

25. Drawing conclusions from tiny samples: n = 3 samples lack statistical power and generalizability.

26. Outlier blindness in correlation: A single extreme point can create a false high correlation.

27. Mixing up Type I and Type II errors: Type I = false positive (α), Type II = false negative (β).

28. Confusing confidence interval with prediction interval: CI bounds the mean; prediction interval bounds individual points.

29. Reporting mean without standard deviation: Without spread, central tendency provides incomplete picture.

30. Failing to check t-test normality: Severe outliers or skew invalidate small-sample parametric t-tests.

13. 22 Output Prediction Challenges

1. Code: mean(c(10, 20, 30, NA))
Prediction: [1] NA (Missing values are contagious in base R).

2. Code: median(c(10, 20, 30, 40))
Prediction: [1] 25 (Average of two middle values: (20 + 30)/2).

3. Code: sd(c(5, 5, 5, 5))
Prediction: [1] 0 (Zero variability when all values are identical).

4. Code: diff(range(c(10, 50, 100)))
Prediction: [1] 90 (100 - 10 = 90).

5. Code: IQR(c(1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8))
Prediction: [1] 3.5 (Q3 = 6.25, Q1 = 2.75 → IQR = 3.5).

6. Code: sum(prop.table(table(c("A","B","A","C"))))
Prediction: [1] 1 (Relative proportions always sum to 1.0).

7. Code: cor(c(1, 2, 3), c(2, 4, 6))
Prediction: [1] 1 (Perfect positive linear relationship).

8. Code: cor(c(1, 2, 3), c(6, 4, 2))
Prediction: [1] -1 (Perfect negative linear relationship).

9. Code: mode(c(1, 2, 2, 3))
Prediction: [1] "numeric" (Returns storage type, not statistical mode!).

10. Code: var(c(10, 20))
Prediction: [1] 50 (Deviations -5 and +5 squared = 25+25 = 50 / (2-1) = 50).

11. Code: sd(c(10, 20))
Prediction: [1] 7.071068 (sqrt(50) = 7.071).

12. Code: weighted.mean(c(100, 50), c(0.8, 0.2))
Prediction: [1] 90 (80 + 10 = 90).

13. Code: fivenum(1:9)[3]
Prediction: [1] 5 (The 3rd element of 5-number summary is the median).

14. Code: t.test(c(10, 10, 10), mu = 10)$p.value
Prediction: NaN or error (Sample variance is 0, t-statistic undefined).

15. Code: sum(is.na(c(1, NA, 3, NA, 5)))
Prediction: [1] 2 (Counts logical TRUE occurrences of missing entries).

16. Code: cor(1:5, (1:5)^2, method = "spearman")
Prediction: [1] 1 (Monotonically increasing ranks preserve perfect rank correlation).

17. Code: quantile(1:100, probs = 0.5)
Prediction: 50% = 50.5 (Median of 1 to 100).

18. Code: mean(c(TRUE, FALSE, TRUE, TRUE))
Prediction: [1] 0.75 (TRUE coerces to 1, FALSE to 0 → 3/4 = 0.75).

19. Code: cov(1:4, 1:4) == var(1:4)
Prediction: [1] TRUE (Covariance of a variable with itself equals its variance).

20. Code: scale(c(10, 20, 30))
Prediction: Mean becomes 0 and standard deviation becomes 1.

21. Code: pnorm(0)
Prediction: [1] 0.5 (50% of standard normal distribution lies below 0).

22. Code: t.test(1:5, mu = 3)$statistic
Prediction: t = 0 (Sample mean is exactly 3; difference from mu is 0).
